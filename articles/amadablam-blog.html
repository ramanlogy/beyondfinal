Complete Guide to Climbing Ama Dablam 2026

No peak in the Himalaya is more photographed. No summit at this altitude demands more technical skill. Ama Dablam at 6,812 metres is Nepal's most beautiful mountain — and one of its most serious. This guide covers everything from permit logistics to the rope-fixed crux sections on the Southwest Ridge, written by guides who have stood on this summit more than sixty times.

Share:

Seen from the trail below Tengboche Monastery, Ama Dablam stops every trekker cold. While Everest is vast and Lhotse is severe, Ama Dablam is simply, unmistakably beautiful — a perfectly proportioned pyramid of ice and grey rock, flanked by two hanging glaciers that give the mountain its name. In Sherpa, ama means mother and dablam means the pendant amulet box carried by Sherpa women. The hanging glaciers are her arms, the summit her amulet, held above the Khumbu Valley for all to see.

At 6,812 metres, Ama Dablam is the highest technically demanding peak that a well-prepared, experienced mountaineer without 8,000-metre experience can realistically aim for. It is not an easy climb. The Southwest Ridge involves fixed rope ascenders, exposed ridge traverses, mixed rock and ice, and sections of genuine commitment where there is no safe retreat option. But it is also one of the most aesthetic climbing routes in the entire Himalayan range — and the summit view, looking straight down on Everest Base Camp and across to the entire Khumbu glacier, is unlike anything in Nepal.

⛏️

This is a Technical Mountaineering Expedition — Not a Trek

Ama Dablam requires prior experience using crampons and ice axe, ascending fixed ropes with a jumar device, and moving confidently on mixed terrain at altitude. It is not suitable for trekkers without mountaineering experience, regardless of fitness level. If you are new to high-altitude climbing, we recommend starting with Island Peak (6,189m) or Mera Peak (6,476m). Jump to the full skills assessment →

Ama Dablam: The Mountain and Its History

Ama Dablam was first summited on 13 May 1961 by a four-man New Zealand and British team — Mike Ward, Barry Bishop, Mike Gill, and Wally Romanes — as part of a Silver Hut expedition studying high-altitude physiology. The Southwest Ridge they established remains the normal route today, essentially unchanged in line though greatly altered by decades of fixed rope installation.

The mountain sits at the junction of the Imja and Dudh Koshi river valleys, directly above the trail between Namche Bazaar and Tengboche — which is why it appears in almost every photograph ever taken on the Everest Base Camp trek. Its elevation (6,812m) places it comfortably above the 6,000-metre threshold that separates serious mountaineering objectives from trekking peaks, and its technical demands make it a legitimate stepping stone toward 7,000-metre and eventually 8,000-metre objectives.

Annually, roughly 300–500 climbers attempt Ama Dablam in the autumn season and 100–200 in spring. Its success rate — approximately 50–60% in normal conditions — reflects both its popularity and its genuine difficulty. Weather windows on the upper ridge are short and unpredictable, and the hanging Dablam glacier above Camp 3 has been in active retreat for decades, requiring regular reassessment of the safe passage beneath it.

The Southwest Ridge — Route Description

The Southwest Ridge is the only regularly climbed route on Ama Dablam and the one used by Beyond Summit Expeditions. It is a long, exposed, and technically varied ridge that divides into four distinct sections between Base Camp and the summit.

BC

Base Camp — 4,570m

Located on a lateral moraine above the Mingbo Valley, a 3–4 hour walk from Pangboche village. Well-established with fixed tents, a cook tent, dining tent, and toilet tents. Phone signal (limited) and emergency satellite communication available. Most expeditions spend 5–7 days here for acclimatisation before the first rotation to Camp 1.

~4,570m3–4 hrs from Pangboche
C1

Camp 1 — Yellow Tower Section — 5,800m

The approach to C1 from base camp traverses loose scree and a short glacier to reach the foot of the ridge. The Yellow Tower — a prominent orange-red rock buttress — is climbed on fixed ropes (Jumar ascenders required). Above the tower, a narrow snow ridge leads to the C1 camp platform. The Yellow Tower is the crux for many climbers on this section and requires confident rope technique.

~5,800mFixed ropes: Yellow Tower5–7 hrs from BC
C2

Camp 2 — Grey Tower / Ridge — 6,000m

From C1, the ridge narrows dramatically. A series of exposed rock steps — the Grey Tower section — requires careful footwork and confident use of fixed ropes on both ascent and descent. The exposure on both sides of the ridge is considerable; this is not a section to negotiate in poor weather. C2 sits on a small rocky platform with room for 3–4 tents. Wind exposure is significant.

~6,000mMost exposed section3–4 hrs from C1
C3

Camp 3 — Below the Dablam — 6,300m

Camp 3 is the highest camp on the mountain, perched on a narrow snow shelf directly beneath the hanging Dablam glacier. The route to C3 from C2 follows the crest of the ridge over mixed terrain, finishing with a steep snow slope. The Dablam glacier above has been actively calving — camp placement and timing of the approach are assessed by guides on a seasonal basis. The summit push begins from C3 at approximately midnight.

~6,300mGlacier objective hazard4–5 hrs from C2
▲

Summit — 6,812m

From C3, the summit push (typically midnight departure) traverses below the Dablam and ascends a steep mixed snow and rock ridge to the final summit pyramid. The uppermost section involves short vertical steps that are fixed in expedition season. The summit itself is a narrow snow ridge with a precipitous drop to the Imja Valley below. Allow 5–7 hours from C3 to summit, 3–4 hours return to C3. Total summit day: 10–12 hours.

The Dablam Glacier — Objective Hazard

The hanging Dablam glacier above Camp 3 has experienced significant calving events in recent years, most notably in 2006 when a collapse destroyed Camp 3 and forced a multi-year reassessment of the upper route. The current C3 position and summit-day timing are designed to minimise exposure beneath the glacier during the warmest hours. Beyond Summit guides reassess glacier conditions at the start of each expedition and maintain the authority to relocate camps or modify summit timing without client override. This is non-negotiable.

Unlike trek itineraries, a climbing expedition schedule is not fixed — it is structured around acclimatisation rotations and weather windows. The itinerary below is Beyond Summit's standard framework. Actual summit day timing will shift based on conditions. The first acclimatisation rotation. Ascend to Camp 1 (5,800m) via the Yellow Tower fixed ropes, spend one night at C1, and descend to base camp the following day. Rest at Base Camp — Recovery Between Rotations

Three full rest days at base camp between rotations. This period is not idle time — it is where the altitude adaptation gained in the first rotation is consolidated. Eating well, hydrating aggressively, and sleeping properly during these days directly determines performance on Rotation 2. Many climbers underestimate rest days; experienced high-altitude climbers treat them as seriously as the rotations themselves.

Second Rotation — Base Camp → Camp 2 → Base Camp

The second and most important acclimatisation rotation. Ascend through C1 to Camp 2 (6,000m), spending one night at C2. This night above 6,000m is the physiological threshold that prepares the body for the summit push. Climbers descend to base camp after one night at C2 — do not be tempted to push to C3 on this rotation. The descent to recover is as important as the ascent to gain altitude.

Rest at Base Camp — Weather Watch

Final rest period at base camp before the summit push. The summit push departs only into a confirmed weather window of at least 3 clear days. This waiting period can extend significantly — patience here is not weakness; it is the primary determinant of success and survival.

Base Camp → Camp 1 → Camp 2

Summit push begins. Move quickly through the now-familiar Yellow Tower and Grey Tower sections, aiming to reach Camp 2 (6,000m) by early afternoon. Rest, eat a full meal, hydrate, and sleep as early as possible. The following day will be among the hardest of your climbing life.

C2: 6,000mEarly afternoon arrival target
Push2

Camp 2 → Camp 3

Move to Camp 3 (6,300m) in the morning. The approach to C3 traverses below the Dablam glacier — timing is chosen by the guide to minimise glacier exposure during warming afternoon hours. Arrive C3 by early afternoon. Melt snow for water, eat whatever you can manage at altitude, and attempt to sleep before the midnight alarm. Prepare all summit gear before lying down.

C3: 6,300mGlacier traverse timing critical

Summit Day — C3 → 6,812m → C2 → Base Camp

Depart C3 at midnight by headtorch. The summit push traverses the upper ridge, ascending the final mixed pyramid in the pre-dawn cold. Summit (6,812m) — a narrow snow crest above the entire Khumbu Valley — typically reached 5–7 hours after departure. Descend immediately: the upper ridge does not allow lingering. Reach C2 by mid-afternoon, continue to base camp for a full return in the same day if conditions allow. The descent is more dangerous than the ascent; full focus is required on fixed ropes when tired.

Weather Windows: The Most Important Variable

The summit success rate on Ama Dablam is determined less by climber fitness than by weather. The stable autumn window typically falls in a 3–4 week corridor from mid-October to mid-November, with 2–4 usable summit windows within it. Spring windows (April–May) are shorter and can end abruptly with pre-monsoon deterioration. Beyond Summit subscribes to a dedicated high-altitude Himalayan meteorological service and will not commit a team to a summit push without a verified 72-hour forecast. Every additional day of waiting at base camp for the right window is a better investment than pushing into uncertain weather.

Climbing Permit and Expedition Costs

Ama Dablam is classified by the Nepal government as a Nepal Mountaineering Association (NMA) peak, which means permits are managed through the NMA rather than the Department of Tourism (which handles 8,000-metre peaks). This makes the permit process faster and cheaper than, for example, an Everest permit — but the costs are still substantial, and must be arranged well in advance of the season.

Best Season for Ama Dablam

Autumn (October to November) — Primary Season

Autumn is the main Ama Dablam climbing season, and for most clients it is the right choice. The weather window is longer, the fixed ropes are installed by early October (usually by resident Sherpa teams who begin rope fixing in late September), and the base camp community — with multiple expeditions in residence — provides a safety net of collective experience and resources. Mid-October to mid-November offers the highest probability of summit weather windows. Expect base camp temperatures of –5°C to –15°C at night.

Spring (April to May)

Spring sees significantly fewer expeditions on the mountain — a meaningful advantage for those who value solitude and quieter fixed rope sections. However, weather windows are shorter and less predictable, pre-monsoon cloud can build rapidly above 6,000m, and the fixed rope installation schedule is less reliable. Spring is recommended for experienced climbers who have already done the route in autumn and understand its rhythms.

Winter and Monsoon Attempts

Winter ascents (December–February) of Ama Dablam have been made by very experienced alpinists but involve extreme cold (–30°C and below at C3), ferocious Jetstream winds above 6,000m, and a near-complete absence of support infrastructure. Monsoon attempts (June–September) are not viable on this route. Beyond Summit does not operate Ama Dablam expeditions outside the autumn and spring windows.

Technical Gear List

The Ama Dablam Southwest Ridge demands a gear list meaningfully different from a trekking or even a high-altitude trekking peak. The fixed rope sections, exposed ridge, and three high camps above 5,800m require mountaineering-grade equipment throughout. Do not attempt to substitute trekking gear for mountaineering gear on this route.

Climbing Hardware

  • Crampons: 12-point, step-in or hybrid binding compatible with your boots. NO strap crampons. Test fit before leaving Kathmandu.
  • Ice axe: 60–70cm general mountaineering axe with spike and adze
  • Jumar (ascender): One per climber — left or right handed per preference. Practice with it before the expedition.
  • Harness: Comfortable for extended wearing at altitude with all layers on. Test with full clothing.
  • Locking carabiners: Minimum 4 HMS lockers + 6 standard lockers
  • Belay device: ATC or equivalent
  • Prussik cord: 6mm × 5m (backup for fixed rope emergency)
  • Helmet: Rockfall is present on the Yellow Tower section
  • Short slings × 2, long sling × 1 for clipping past fixed rope anchors

High Altitude Clothing

  • High-altitude boots: Double or triple plastic, or modern insulated mountaineering boot rated to –40°C. NOT trekking boots. Recommended: La Sportiva Olympus Mons Cube, Scarpa Phantom 8000, or equivalent.
  • Down suit or equivalent layering: A one-piece expedition down suit (800+ fill, rated to –30°C) is strongly recommended for Camp 3 and the summit push
  • Expedition mitts: Outer waterproof mitts over liner gloves. Your hands must remain functional on fixed ropes at –20°C.
  • Goggles: High-altitude UV goggles (not sunglasses) for glacier sections and summit day
  • Neoprene face mask or balaclava for wind protection on the upper ridge

Sleeping and Camp

  • Sleeping bag: Down, rated to –30°C minimum. Beyond Summit provides a high camp tent at C1, C2, and C3; sleeping bags are personal equipment.
  • Sleeping mat: Insulated inflatable pad — foam alone is insufficient at C3
  • Headtorch: With fresh lithium batteries + 2 full spare sets. Your summit day starts at midnight.
  • High-capacity power bank: Phone and camera batteries drain fast in cold
What Beyond Summit Provides

All fixed ropes on the route (replaced fresh each season), high camp tents at C1, C2, and C3, a base camp cook tent and full catering, camp kitchen equipment, emergency supplemental oxygen (one set per team, not per climber — for genuine emergency use), a satellite phone, a pulse oximeter, and a Gamow bag at base camp. Frequently Asked Questions

I've done EBC trek and some scrambling — is Ama Dablam achievable?

Probably not yet, and we'd encourage honesty here. The EBC trek reaches 5,364m but involves no technical climbing. Scrambling on rock is useful background but does not prepare you for ascending a fixed rope on a vertical tower at 5,700m with 800 metres of exposure below. A more realistic pathway is: Island Peak (6,189m) to gain crampon and Jumar experience, then Mera Peak (6,476m) for altitude above 6,000m, then Ama Dablam. That two-season preparation turns Ama Dablam from a dangerous gamble into an achievable goal.

How many Sherpa guides will be on the expedition?

Beyond Summit operates at a minimum ratio of one climbing Sherpa per two clients on Ama Dablam. Our lead guide (typically with 30+ personal Ama Dablam summits) leads the team. Additional support Sherpas carry loads to high camps and fix supplemental rope where required. You are never alone above base camp.

What is the evacuation procedure if someone falls ill above Camp 2?

Emergency evacuation from above C2 is by helicopter to a safe landing zone near base camp or Pangboche, then onwards to Kathmandu if required. This is why helicopter rescue insurance is a condition of booking. Our guides carry a satellite communicator and can call in a helicopter from Lukla or Namche within 2–4 hours in good weather. The Gamow bag at base camp can treat acute mountain sickness before evacuation if helicopter delays occur. Our guides are trained in wilderness first aid and high-altitude medicine.

Has anyone died on Ama Dablam?

Yes. Ama Dablam has claimed the lives of climbers, primarily through falls on the exposed ridge sections and weather-related incidents. The mountain demands respect proportional to its beauty. The majority of serious incidents involve either inadequately prepared climbers, poor weather decisions, or objective hazard from the Dablam glacier. Beyond Summit's record is clean — and we intend to keep it that way by being selective about who we take and conservative about when we push.
